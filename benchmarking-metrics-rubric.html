Benchmarking Metrics Rubric

Nine metrics across two clusters. Cluster A captures what companies built (architecture state); Cluster B captures how they work (operating model, definition mechanism, and transformation enablers). Each metric carries a fixed Leader / Strong / Emerging / Lagging bar, set before any company is researched, so every rating stays comparable. Metric 8 is a qualitative lens rather than a scored bar.

Cluster A — What They Built  (6 metrics)
1. Platform Reuse & Reuse Economics
2. Modularity
3. Configuration Mgmt & Digital Thread
4. SDV Maturity & HW–SW Integration
5. OTA
6. Integrated Services
METRIC 1Platform Reuse & Reuse Economics

Systemic reuse of physical and informational assets across programs — and whether that reuse is real (as-is, Adopted/Managed) or nominal (Modified/net-new dressed up as "shared platform"). Captures both the architecture fact (do parts carry over) and the economics (is that carryover cheap, genuine reuse, or expensive re-engineering per variant).

REUSE TAXONOMY — per Valerdi (applies to Metric 1)

AdoptedReused as-is, same application. No physical change, no re-validation. Highest-value reuse.
ManagedReused as-is, but a new application. No physical change, but requires context re-validation.
ModifiedPhysically changed for the new use. Real engineering effort & re-qualification.
Value hierarchy for scoring: Adopted > Managed > Modified > Net-new. A Leader maximizes Adopted + Managed and treats Modified / net-new as deliberate exceptions.
Leader
>70% part/architecture commonality across the family; majority of reuse is Adopted/Managed (as-is); net-new minimized; active rationalization
Strong
50–70% commonality; reuse mostly Adopted/Managed; Modified used purposefully, not by default
Emerging
25–50% commonality; reuse frequently Modified/re-validated; ad hoc governance
Lagging
<25% commonality, or "shared platform" claims that are nominal only; defaults to Modified/net-new; part proliferation
Why it matters →Directly maps to Ford's Big Shift #4 (Platform Thinking & Economies of Scale). A company can look strong on raw commonality % while re-validating almost everything per variant (nominal reuse); this metric catches both. Platform Thinking & Economies of Scale
Expected Ford placement: Lagging — "part-first" proliferation, weak carryover governance. Real reuse-quality baseline pending Step 5.
Source: cross-company commonality evidence (Toyota TNGA, VW MQB, Hyundai IMA, Tesla gigacasting, BYD CTB/8-in-1 — see Evidence Detail); Valerdi reuse taxonomy for quality classification; McKinsey cost-of-complexity modeling (part standardization cut total cost ~3% despite higher unit cost).
METRIC 2Modularity

How cleanly the product decomposes into independent, recombinable modules with defined interfaces — the architectural precondition for reuse, as distinct from Metric 1's outcome (whether reuse actually happens). A company can be modular but under-exploit it, or claim commonality without real decoupling underneath; this metric isolates the structural fact.

Leader
Modules truly decoupled & interchangeable across segments/body styles; defined interfaces; new variants assembled from a catalog
Strong
Most subsystems modular; some segment-specific bespoke integration remains
Emerging
Modularity exists on architecture diagrams; few modules are actually cross-segment interchangeable in practice
Lagging
Bespoke, integrated design per model; little/no module reuse across segments
Why it matters →Pairs with Metric 1 to separate two different failure modes: low commonality despite good architecture (execution/governance problem) vs. low commonality because the architecture itself won't allow it (design problem) — Ford needs to know which one it has. Platform Thinking & Economies of Scale
Expected Ford placement: Lagging / Emerging — per Big Shifts primer, "siloed one-program-at-a-time execution... high part complexity."
Source: BYD 8-in-1 powertrain spanning 3 body styles on e-Platform 3.0; Hyundai IMA >80 common modules across every segment; VW MQB standardized engine-mounting core across drivetrain types; Toyota TNGA standardized seat-height positions across body styles — see Evidence Detail.
In the companies scored so far, high modularity and high reuse move together. The two ratings are expected to diverge for a company whose architecture allows sharing that governance doesn't yet exploit — worth watching for as research continues.
METRIC 3Configuration Management & Digital Thread

Degree to which product definition — requirements → BOM → 3D → cost — lives in one connected, live, machine-readable model with disciplined variant governance, versus siloed static artifacts and ad hoc change control. A live, connected model is what configuration-management leadership looks like in practice — there's no way to govern variants well without one.

Leader
Single live, data-centric model; digital twin; real-time cost/3D/part linkage; variant changes propagate automatically
Strong
Integrated PLM backbone; most authoring connected & structured; variant governance mostly systematic
Emerging
Partial integration; several authoring silos; manual reconciliation; variant control inconsistent
Lagging
Document-centric; disconnected artifacts; tribal knowledge; variants tracked manually
Why it matters →This is Ford's core pain (44+ systems, 100+ disconnected artifacts) made measurable — and the direct target of a single governed PDL. Making the Invisible VisibleCentralized Product Definition
Expected Ford placement: Lagging — 8+ siloed authoring sources; document-centric culture.
Source: digital-twin EBIT uplift of up to ~12%; Gen-AI R&D productivity gains (30–70% on requirements/test work); NIO 3DEXPERIENCE/ENOVIA case (3-hr → 15-min model load) — see Evidence Detail.
NIO is currently the primary evidence source for this metric's Leader/Strong tiers — a single vendor case study, not yet independently corroborated across multiple companies.
METRIC 4SDV Maturity & HW–SW Integration

Software-defined-vehicle capability: a unified vehicle OS spanning hardware generations, centralized zonal/domain compute, and OTA-updatable coverage across most of the vehicle — versus a distributed legacy-ECU architecture. Scored as one composite, matching how independent analysts (Gartner, S&P Global Mobility) already treat software-defined-vehicle capability.

Leader
Unified OS across hardware generations; centralized compute; OTA reaches nearly all ECUs; top-tier on independent SDV indices
Strong
Centralized compute for major domains; OTA covers most software, some legacy ECUs remain fragmented; upper-tier on at least one index
Emerging
Some domain consolidation; OTA limited to infotainment/telematics; hardware and software architected largely separately
Lagging
Distributed legacy-ECU architecture (dozens of siloed controllers); OTA rare or absent; bottom tier or unranked on SDV indices
Why it matters →The core technical enabler behind Automated Authoring and Purpose-Driven Innovation — without a unified, software-addressable architecture, feature-first definition (Metric 9) has nothing to run on. Automated AuthoringPurpose-Driven Innovation
Expected Ford placement: Lagging — per Gartner's 2025 Digital Automaker Index, legacy players averaged 33.8% vs. Tesla's 79.3%, with Ford named among those needing to catch up.
Source: Gartner 2025 Digital Automaker Index; S&P Global Mobility SDV Capability Framework (NIO ET9 rated Level 5); IMD Future Readiness Indicator 2025/2026 — see Evidence Detail.
BMW's production digital-twin evidence sits under Metric 3 (Configuration Management & Digital Thread), not here — it's a manufacturing-system capability, not a vehicle software-architecture one.
METRIC 5Over-the-Air Updates

Breadth (how much of the software stack, beyond infotainment) and cadence of over-the-air updates — the delivery channel that makes continuous improvement and remote remediation possible without a dealer visit. Distinct from Metric 4 (the architecture that makes OTA technically possible) and Metric 9 (whether the product itself is structured around monetizable features to push through that channel).

Leader
OTA reaches powertrain/ADAS/safety-critical systems, not just infotainment; frequent cadence; used for real recalls & remediation
Strong
OTA covers most non-safety-critical systems; regular cadence; feature updates common
Emerging
OTA limited to infotainment/connectivity; irregular cadence
Lagging
No meaningful OTA; software changes require a dealer visit or physical connection
Why it matters →The delivery mechanism that makes feature-first monetization (Metric 9) commercially real, not just architecturally possible. Informed Decision Making
Expected Ford placement: Emerging — some OTA capability exists (e.g. infotainment/connected services) but not yet at safety-critical-system depth; to be confirmed at Step 5.
Source: Tesla 2019 10-K ($1.73B deferred revenue tied to OTA features); NIO and BYD OTA disclosures — see Evidence Detail.
Current evidence for this metric overlaps with Metrics 6 and 9 (same primary source — Tesla's 10-K and Morgan Stanley's estimate). The three measure genuinely different things — channel, commercial layer, and product-definition mechanism — but need independent corroboration before all three ratings can be trusted as separately verified.
METRIC 6Integrated Services

Breadth and monetization depth of connected/subscription services layered on top of the core vehicle (e.g. autonomy subscriptions, connected insurance, fleet/data services) — the commercial layer that OTA (Metric 5) and SDV maturity (Metric 4) make possible, but don't guarantee gets monetized.

Leader
Multiple monetized service lines with disclosed recurring revenue; services structurally embedded in the product roadmap
Strong
At least one clear monetized service line with modest disclosed revenue
Emerging
Services exist but are largely bundled/free; little standalone monetization
Lagging
No meaningful connected-service monetization; connectivity is a cost center, not a revenue line
Why it matters →The commercial proof-point that closes the loop on the whole Digital Backbone thesis — the "Value" column of the Proven Value panel. Making the Invisible VisiblePurpose-Driven Innovation
Expected Ford placement: Lagging / Emerging — to be baselined at Step 5.
Source: Tesla 2019 10-K; Morgan Stanley (A. Jonas) software gross-profit estimate — see Evidence Detail.
This metric currently shares its primary proof point with Metrics 5 and 9. Conceptually distinct — this is the commercial/P&L outcome, not the delivery channel or the product structure — but needs an independent source before the rating can be trusted as separately corroborated.
Cluster B — How They Work  (3 metrics)
METRIC 7Speed / Time-to-Value

Elapsed time from concept freeze to Start-of-Production (SOP), plus how much of validation is virtual vs. physical-prototype-dependent. Measures how fast the PD system converts intent into producible value.

Leader
<24 mo concept-to-SOP; virtual-first validation (>60% simulation)
Strong
24–36 mo; heavy simulation use; disciplined process
Emerging
36–48 mo; mixed physical/virtual validation
Lagging
>48 mo; physical-prototype-dependent; serial
Why it matters →Directly quantifies speed-to-market. Benchmark disruptors reach ~21 months concept-to-pilot vs. up to ~48 months for legacy OEMs. Informed Decision Making
Expected Ford placement: Lagging / Emerging — serial, prototype-heavy cycles.
Source: McKinsey OEM development-cycle analyses; zonal-architecture studies (20–30% faster concept-to-mass-production).
Part of the evidence used for NIO reflects review/model-load speed (an enabler metric), not full concept-to-SOP program time. Treat as directional pending a dedicated time-to-market source.

What actually drove each transformation to succeed — not a performance score, but a coded archetype. Captured as one primary + secondary driver per company so Ford targets the real lever, not just the visible mechanism.

Capital / Finance-Forced

Source: qualitative coding from company case studies & transformation literature; validated with Deloitte–Ford team (not a numeric benchmark).

METRIC 9Feature-Driven Definition

Degree to which product definition is organized around customer-perceivable features/functions that carry through requirements → architecture → variant management, versus a “part-first” structure built on components and surrogates. Measures the mechanism behind Area 6 — not the software outcome (that is Metric 4), but whether the PDL itself is feature-structured.

Leader
Feature model is the backbone of definition; features traced end-to-end; features monetized (OTA / feature-on-demand)
Strong
Formal feature-based PLE in place; most variants derived from a governed feature catalog
Emerging
Feature thinking in software only; hardware definition still part-first
Lagging
Part-first definition on static PDLs; features implicit; surrogate/clinic-driven
Why it matters →This is Area 6’s From→To (“part-first” → “feature-first”) made scoreable — and it is externally benchmarked: Gartner’s 2025 Digital Automaker Index ranks 24 OEMs on software-and-feature maturity, placing digital natives far ahead of legacy players and naming Ford among those needing to catch up. Purpose-Driven Innovation
Expected Ford placement: Lagging — part-first PDLs, feature intent captured via surveys/surrogates rather than a governed feature model.
Anchor (method, peer-reviewed): feature-driven systems-engineering procedure, Granrath et al., INCOSE Systems Engineering (Wiley), 2021. Named case: feature-based PLE at General Motors (BigLever / SPLC 2017 — vendor/academic).
